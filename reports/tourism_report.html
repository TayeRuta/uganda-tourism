Uganda's tourism: who comes, when, and what goes unsold

Uganda counts about 1.4 million tourist arrivals a year, but only a fifth come on holiday. This report separates holiday tourism from regional travel, follows national park visits and gorilla permits month by month, compares Uganda's recovery with its peers, and shows where the official numbers disagree.

Key takeaways

  1. Most "tourists" are not on holiday. In 2024, 19% came for leisure; the rest visited friends and family or came for business, study or health. Nine in ten came from Africa, and Kenya and Rwanda alone sent 63%. High
  2. The arrival counts are not comparable over time. The system changed in 2019, and the published 2019 figures range from 657,000 to 1.54 million depending on source and definition. High
  3. National parks had a record 2024, but the growth is domestic. Visits were 35% above 2019; foreign non-resident visitors were only 4% above. Ugandan students and East Africans drove the rest. High
  4. Visitors come in July and August. Those two months bring 29% of park visits, and about 90% of gorilla permits sell; March brings 5% of visits. The link to rainfall is weak; holiday calendars may matter as much. Medium
  5. Gorilla capacity outgrew demand. Permits rose 26% in 2024 but sales only 7%, so the share sold fell from 69% to 58%. 55% of unsold permits were in the four wettest months. High
  6. Visitor earnings recovered more slowly than the neighbours': 94% of 2019 in 2023, against 100% for Kenya, 123% for Rwanda and 130% for Tanzania. Medium
  7. The 2026 low-season gorilla discount shows no clear effect yet. April–May sales rose about 4% more than the trend, against the 33% needed to keep revenue level; but the first season is too early to judge. Early

ReadingPark visits reached 437,000 in 2024, 35% above 2019. Foreign non-resident visitors (160,000) were only 4% above their 2019 level; Ugandan students (120,000) and East African residents (106,000) drove the growth. Murchison Falls, Queen Elizabeth and Bwindi take two-thirds to three-quarters of all visits every year. Growth is uneven: Semliki, Queen Elizabeth and Murchison grew most since 2019, while Kidepo Valley, Rwenzori and Mount Elgon shrank. In January–June 2026 visits fell 8%, entirely at Queen Elizabeth (−40%); all other parks together grew 5%. The source gives no reason for Queen Elizabeth's fall.

ReadingJuly and August bring 29% of the year's park visits, and about 90% of gorilla permits sold in 2024; March brings 5% of visits. The quietest months include the south-west's wettest, but August is busy despite rain, and across the year the link to rainfall is weak (rank correlation −0.18 for park visits, −0.48 for gorilla bookings, neither significant). The peak matches European and North American summer holidays at least as well. Total arrivals barely vary by month, because most arrivals are regional visitors.

ReadingCapacity rose 26% in 2024 while sales rose 7%, so the extra permits went mostly unsold, and most of those were in the wet months. Demand in the low season, not the number of permits, is the constraint. In July and August 2023 recorded sales exceed the listed permits by up to a third (134% in August), an inconsistency in the source that also inflates 2023's 69%. Chimpanzee tracking sold 44% of permits in 2024.
06 · The low-season test

Did the 2026 discount fill the wet months?

On 26 February 2026 the Uganda Wildlife Authority cut the foreign non-resident gorilla permit from US$800 to US$600 for April, May and November, as reported by several tour operators (not yet in a published tariff). Permits available in April and May were almost the same in 2025 and 2026, so sales can be compared directly, against the full-price months before them and against a year with no discount. June is left out because park visits fell sharply that month for other reasons.

ReadingApril–May 2026 sales grew about 4 points faster than January–March, but in 2025, with no discount, the gap was about the same (5 points). The discount would need sales a third higher to keep permit revenue level; the first season delivered about 4% above trend. This is an early read: the discount was announced about five weeks before April, many trips were booked earlier at full price, and operators report that only part of the permits are released at the lower price. November 2026 and the 2027 low season will be the real test.
07 · Shocks

COVID-19 hit hard; later shocks left little mark nationally

National park visits by month, thousands
Hover a month · shaded: Ebola outbreak, Sep 2022 – Jan 2023
ReadingCOVID-19 cut park visits by 69% in 2020; they passed 2019 levels only in 2022. The Ebola outbreak of September 2022 – January 2023 left no clear mark: park visits in October–December 2022 ran at the same ratio to 2023 as the rest of that year. Queen Elizabeth National Park, where two foreign tourists and their guide were killed in October 2023, had a record 2024 (+33%), but its visits fell 40% in January–June 2026, for reasons the source does not give. National and annual figures can hide short or local effects, so these are weak tests.
08 · What it means

Fill the low season, grow holiday travel, fix the counts

Operators and investors

The low season is the opportunity

  • About 30,000 gorilla permits went unsold in 2024, worth up to US$24 million at the foreign rate, over half in March–May and November.
  • July–August is about 90% sold; most room to grow is in the shoulder and wet months, but the 2026 price cut has not yet shown it can fill them.
  • The domestic and student market is now the fastest-growing park segment.
Government and partners

Measure holiday tourism directly

  • Leisure visitors (265,000 in 2024) are a better target than total arrivals, most of which are regional travel.
  • Overseas arrivals are small (137,000) and fell from Europe in 2024.
  • Recovery lags Kenya, Rwanda and Tanzania on comparable earnings.
Statisticians

One consistent series

  • Reconcile the 2019 counts and publish a back-cast series across the switch to PISCES.
  • Publish purpose of visit for 2017–2019.
  • Fix the July–August gorilla permit figures and extend the hotel survey to safari lodges.

09 · Methods and limits

How this was done

Method

  • Tables extracted from the Ministry of Tourism's Statistical Abstract 2025 and the Uganda Bureau of Statistics' Statistical Abstracts 2017 and 2020, each checked against its printed totals
  • Benchmark from UN Tourism inbound travel receipts (balance of payments), 2015–2023
  • Seasonality as average shares of the year by month; rank correlation with regional rainfall from the rainfall project
  • Shocks compared with the same months of the following year
  • Discount test: growth in April–May against January–March, compared with a year without a discount

Limits

  • Arrival counts change basis in 2019; 2017–2019 purpose of visit is not published
  • Most series are annual and short (2012–2024 at best), so few formal tests are possible
  • Hotel occupancy appears to cover mainly urban hotels and shows little seasonality
  • Gorilla permit sales in July–August exceed listed permits in the source
  • Unsold permit value is an upper bound at the foreign non-resident price

Sources. Ministry of Tourism, Wildlife and Antiquities, Statistical Abstract 2025 and Tourism Statistics January–June 2026. Uganda Bureau of Statistics, Statistical Abstracts 2017 and 2020.
